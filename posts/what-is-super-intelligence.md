The switch from **AI** to **SI** is not just a branding argument. It is a signal that the language around machine intelligence is moving faster than most organizations can absorb.

On September 29, 2026, the White House issued an executive order directing the U.S. executive branch to use **“Super Intelligence”** and **“SI”** in place of **“Artificial Intelligence”** and **“AI”** in official correspondence, public communications, websites, reports, policy documents, and other non-statutory documents. The order says the new terms currently mean the same technologies covered by the existing statutory definition of artificial intelligence, while also directing officials to propose a more specific federal definition within 60 days.

That is the important part. For now, the order changes the label more than the legal substance. But labels matter. They shape budgets, standards, procurement, public expectations, and the words your customers type into search.

The disciplined response is not to chase the new acronym blindly. It is to understand what changed, what did not, and where your operating system needs to get sharper.

## What is Super Intelligence?

**Super Intelligence, or SI, now has two meanings that operators need to keep separate.** In the new federal language, SI is a replacement term for the technologies previously called artificial intelligence. In technical and frontier-lab language, superintelligence usually means systems much more capable than humans across broad domains.

That distinction matters because the same words are being used at two different altitude levels.

The executive order says that, for implementation purposes, “Super Intelligence” and “SI” mean the technologies and systems encompassed by the existing federal definition of artificial intelligence. NIST has already updated its public AI division page to say it is incorporating the term “super intelligence” as directed by the September 29 order.

That is an administrative shift.

By contrast, OpenAI has used “superintelligence” to mean future systems dramatically more capable than AGI. In its governance note, OpenAI described superintelligence as systems beyond even artificial general intelligence and argued that such systems would need special coordination, audits, compliance testing, deployment restrictions, and public oversight.

Google DeepMind’s 2026 report, “From AGI to ASI,” uses artificial general superintelligence for a system more intelligent and cognitively capable than large organizations of humans. Safe Superintelligence Inc. uses SSI as the company’s entire mission and product roadmap. Meta frames personal superintelligence as a personal agent that understands your goals and helps you act through everyday devices.

So when you see SI, ask one question first: **is this a policy label for today’s AI systems, or a technical claim about future capability?**

## Why is the terminology changing now?

**The terminology is changing because frontier AI has outgrown the public’s old mental model of “software that imitates intelligence.”** The order argues that today’s systems do more than automate discrete pieces of human intelligence; they amplify human ingenuity and unlock new forms of creativity across science, medicine, and other domains.

That is the government’s case for the word change.

There is a reasonable point underneath the rhetoric. The phrase “artificial intelligence” has become too broad to be useful. It can mean autocomplete, a chatbot, a coding model, a voice agent, a fraud detector, a synthetic image tool, a business workflow agent, or a future system capable of improving its own research cycle.

When one term covers everything, it stops guiding decisions.

But “Super Intelligence” creates its own problem. It raises expectations. It can make today’s uneven tools sound more sovereign, more capable, and more reliable than they are. A scheduling agent that drops context is not superintelligence. A browser automation that clicks the wrong button is not superintelligence. A model that produces confident but uncited work is still a system that needs supervision.

The honest catch: the new language may help government communications point toward the frontier, but it can also inflate ordinary software in the market.

For operators, that means the work gets more sober, not less. More specific scopes. Better logs. Clearer human approval. Measurable outcomes. Less reverence for the label.

## Is SI the same as artificial superintelligence?

**No, not always. SI in the current federal order is not automatically the same as artificial superintelligence, or ASI.** The order explicitly ties SI to the existing statutory definition of AI unless Congress or future presidential action changes it.

Artificial superintelligence usually means something narrower and more extreme: a system that surpasses human capability broadly, not just one that performs a single task well.

Google DeepMind’s AGI levels paper placed artificial superintelligence at the highest combination of generality and performance: superhuman general capability. Its later “From AGI to ASI” report describes a transition from human-level AGI to systems more capable than large human organizations.

That is not the same as “we renamed the current federal AI program.”

This is where public confusion will appear. A vendor can now say “SI” and sound aligned with government language while still selling ordinary AI automation. A lab can say “superintelligence” and mean a potential future system with civilization-level implications. A policymaker can say “SI” and mean the old AI definition with a new frame.

The word alone will not tell you which one you are dealing with. The useful questions are:

- What capability is being claimed?
- What evidence proves it?
- What decisions can the system make without a human?
- What permissions does it have?
- What happens when it is wrong?
- Who owns the final outcome?

That is the operating layer beneath the terminology.

## What changes for businesses right now?

**For most businesses, the immediate change is language awareness, not a full rebrand.** If you sell to federal agencies, work in regulated procurement, publish compliance materials, or track NIST guidance, you should monitor the SI language closely. If you sell to customers who still search for “AI automation,” “AI agents,” or “AI marketing,” do not erase the terms they understand.

Search demand still matters. Customers do not update their vocabulary the day a memo lands. They search with the words already in their head.

The practical move is to build a translation layer:

- Use **AI** where customers still search for AI.
- Use **SI** when discussing the executive order, federal terminology, NIST, or future-facing policy.
- Use **superintelligence** carefully when referring to frontier capability or ASI-level discussion.
- Define the term the first time it appears.
- Avoid claiming that today’s workflow tools are superintelligent unless you can prove the capability.

For BBH-style operations, this is the same principle behind sane [agentic systems](/agentic-systems): the label is less important than the working control surface. Can the agent do the job? Can a human inspect the work? Are permissions scoped? Are handoffs visible? Are logs good enough to reconstruct what happened?

If the answer is no, the system is not ready just because the acronym changed.

## What should operators do this week?

**Treat the AI-to-SI shift as a governance trigger.** Do not start by rewriting every page. Start by tightening the places where terminology, authority, and evidence meet.

First, audit public language. Find the pages, proposals, decks, and policies where “AI” appears. Mark which ones are customer-facing, compliance-facing, and internal. Customer-facing pages may still need AI language for clarity and search. Compliance-facing pages may need SI language sooner if they reference federal materials.

Second, create a short house definition. Something like: “We use AI for customer search clarity, SI when discussing current federal terminology, and superintelligence/ASI only for systems exceeding broad human capability.” This keeps the team from improvising a new meaning in every document.

Third, review your agent governance. Stronger language around intelligence should push stronger controls around autonomy. Revisit permissions, approval gates, model-routing decisions, human handoff surfaces, and receipt trails. If an agent can send messages, spend money, alter records, or contact customers, the review line should be explicit.

Fourth, connect language to outcomes. If your business uses AI for lead response, content operations, CRM hygiene, research, reporting, or paid-search workflows, measure the work. Did the system reduce missed leads? Did it improve response time? Did it create fewer review burdens, or more? This is where [SMB marketing](/smb-marketing) and automation either become leverage or become another dashboard to babysit.

Fifth, keep watching the standards layer. NIST says its SI work includes measurement science, testing and evaluation, guidance, standards, trustworthy deployment, and testing, evaluation, verification, and validation. That is the boring infrastructure that will matter after the acronym debate fades.

## The BBH take

**The move from AI to SI is a language shift before it is a capability shift.** It points toward a real frontier: more capable systems, more agentic workflows, and eventually systems that may exceed individual humans or even large organizations in broad cognitive work. But the name does not remove the need for discipline.

If anything, it raises the standard.

The more powerful the language becomes, the more concrete your operations need to be. Define terms. Scope authority. Verify claims. Keep humans responsible for direction. Build systems that leave receipts.

Do not get hypnotized by the new acronym. The useful question is not whether the market says AI or SI this quarter. The useful question is whether your organization is becoming more capable because of the systems it is building.

That is the preparation that compounds.
